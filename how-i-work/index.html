Engineering and management style

How I work

LinkedIn lists where I’ve worked. This page is about how: the way I build software and the way I lead the people who build it. Each point comes with an example of where it has actually shown up.

Shayan Zafar Ahmad · Updated October 2026

The short version

Small, checkable steps. Numbers and decisions you can trace. Technology simple enough for the next person to own. Delivery rhythms that make good outcomes the default, and people who grow into bigger roles.

Part 1

As an engineer

I’ve written production software for 14 years, mostly C#, T-SQL and TypeScript on Azure, and I still build: at work in design reviews and code, and on my own projects in Python. A few habits run through all of it.

I build in phases, and each phase has a check

I avoid big-bang releases. I break work into phases small enough to finish, define up front what proves each one works, and start the next only when that check passes. Each finished phase ships.

In practice ThirteenF went from a prototype to eight working phases, each gated by its own check and pushed as it passed. At MitraTech I rebuilt the SDLC around the same idea: technical planning first, so risk surfaces before code is written.

Every number should be traceable

If a system shows a number, I want to know where it came from and be able to rebuild it from source. When data fails a sanity check, I surface the issue rather than show a number that might be wrong.

In practice ThirteenF never edits the raw SEC files; every figure is derived from them and carries its as-of date and source, and share totals that fail an implied-price check are flagged instead of shown. Edge cases such as CUSIP changes, managers switching identifiers and incomplete filings are handled explicitly and covered by tests, including checks against published reference numbers.

I choose simple, proven technology

Given two ways to build something, I choose the one the next engineer can understand and maintain.

In practice ThirteenF runs on Python, DuckDB and FastAPI, with server-rendered pages and server-drawn SVG charts instead of a front-end framework. This site is plain HTML and CSS with no build step.

Consistency is designed in, not retrofitted

Shared foundations, from design tokens to a branching strategy to a release calendar, are cheaper to agree on early than to enforce later.

In practice ThirteenF had a design system (colour tokens, components and writing rules) and finished screen designs before the first page was built. At MitraTech I replaced a conflicting multi-team release schedule with one monthly cycle and a cleaner git branching strategy.

I start from the user’s question

Strong requirements start from a real user’s question, answered end to end.

In practice At DraftKings we wrote a C# client against our own real-time odds API to test the partner documentation from the partner’s side. ThirteenF’s money-flow pages started as one question: where is the money moving for this stock?

Security and reliability are part of the feature

Uptime, observability and security debt belong on the same backlog as features, not after them.

In practice At Ontario Health my teams kept an emergency triage system highly available on Azure, using deployment slots and Application Insights. At MitraTech I prioritized security debt from Snyk reports and pen-test findings and added Datadog monitoring.

Part 2

As a manager

I’ve led engineers and team leads since 2019, in healthcare, sports betting, retail and HR software. I see my job as making good delivery the default, and helping people grow along the way.

I make the delivery system predictable

Teams do their best work with a clear plan and a steady rhythm: a written technical plan, one release cycle, and delivery metrics everyone can see.

In practice At MitraTech: technical planning before code, best-practice CI/CD and a monthly release cycle. At DraftKings I owned the roadmap, backlog and timelines, and tracked delivery health through velocity, burndown and churn.

Distributed teams need deliberate rhythms

Across time zones, alignment doesn’t happen by accident; it has to be designed into the week.

In practice My twelve engineers span India, US East and West, and Mexico. A recurring cross-time-zone technical sync cut delivery uncertainty and raised code quality.

Interviews should look like the job

I hire for how people actually work: how they read code, solve a problem and reason about a design.

In practice I built a hiring process around code review, a coding exercise and a technical design discussion, plus an onboarding framework for new developers. I’ve hired team leads, senior developers and QA for new teams at Staples Canada, and senior developers at Ontario Health.

Growth is part of the job

I measure part of my success by the growth of the people on my team.

In practice At DraftKings I owned hiring, performance, one-on-ones and career growth for my team, and promoted an intermediate developer to senior and a lead developer to manager.

I translate engineering into business terms

Executives need clear plans, costs and trade-offs they can act on.

In practice Roadmaps, capacity planning and initiative costing with executives at DraftKings and Staples Canada; vendor and licence audits that cut tooling spend at MitraTech.

In a crisis, narrow the focus

When the stakes rise, I focus the team on what matters most and keep critical systems running.

In practice During COVID-19 at Ontario Health, my teams built an API to track infection data and adapted the emergency triage application to pandemic demand, while keeping a highly available system running for Ontario hospitals.

Part 3

Where AI fits

I treat AI like any new capability on a team: prove it on real work, put guardrails around it, measure it, then scale it.

At work

At MitraTech I led engineering-wide adoption: procuring licences, setting guardrails and best practices, integrating Devin for code scanning and metrics, and running proofs of concept with GitHub Copilot and Devin on real tech debt and features before rolling them out.

In my own work

I use AI coding tools on my own projects with the same discipline I expect from a team: a written build plan with a check for every phase, a design system, clear project rules, and tests before anything ships.
